_**Elevator Pitch**_
Heat waves don't hit cities evenly. One block can sit under a stationary heat dome while the air a few streets over stays breathable. And the people on the hottest blocks are often the least able to escape it: an elderly resident alone in a top-floor apartment with no air conditioning, a family that can't afford to run a fan all night, someone with no home at all.

City-wide heat alerts are too slow and too broad for that. Opening a cooling center doesn't help if the people who need it most are miles away.

**SHADE**, the Smart Heat Alert & Dispatch Engine, fixes that. It combines live weather data, urban heat-island readings, building age, and socio-economic data into one map that shows not just where it's hottest, but where heat is most likely to hurt someone.

When a heat spike starts to form, SHADE flags the highest-risk blocks before the first 911 call. Emergency teams can send hydration and medical units, route wellness-check volunteers to specific buildings, and redirect transit to shuttle residents to cooling hubs. Limited resources go to the most exposed people first.

_We can't stop the climate from changing overnight. But with SHADE, no neighbor has to face the heat alone._


_**Full Pitch**_
The seasons stopped being predictable years ago, but the sudden, localized extremes—the anomalies—were what truly demanded our attention. It was no longer just a slow, generalized warming; it was the sudden spikes. Neighborhoods baking under invisible, stationary heat domes while a few streets over, the air remained entirely breathable. It was winter plunges that shattered pipes and froze uninsulated homes overnight. We realized that broad, city-wide climate policies were too slow and too blunt to save lives in real-time. We initiated this effort because the margin between discomfort and tragedy had vanished, and the danger had become devastatingly precise.

When the grid falters or the mercury spikes, the true cost of a temperature anomaly is always paid by the most vulnerable among us. For an elderly resident living alone in a top-floor apartment without air conditioning, a three-day heatwave transforms their home into a suffocating trap. For low-income families unable to afford a sudden surge in energy bills forces agonizing choices between putting food on the table and keeping the air conditioning running. For the unhoused, these extreme shifts are immediate, life-threatening crises. In the micro-climates of asphalt, concrete, and systemic neglect, extreme weather isn't an inconvenience—it acts as a predator.
 
That is exactly why we built our predictive mapping tool: Urban Heat Resilience Planner. We knew that simply opening cooling centers wasn't enough if the people who desperately needed them were miles away. Our system ingests real-time meteorological data, urban heat island metrics, and socio-economic overlays to create a living, breathing map of a climate crisis as it unfolds. It doesn’t just show us where the weather is breaking records; it shows us exactly where the weather is threatening lives.

By illuminating these danger zones, the tool allows us to flip our emergency response from reactive to proactively defensive. When a thermal anomaly begins to form, the map highlights the exact city blocks facing the highest intersecting risks. Before the first emergency call even comes in, we are already dispatching mobile hydration and medical units, routing wellness-check volunteers to specific high-risk apartment buildings, and redirecting transit to shuttle residents to climate-controlled community hubs. It allows us to target our compassion with pinpoint accuracy, ensuring that our finite resources are poured directly into the hardest-hit, most critically exposed areas first.

Stabilizing the climate is one of the greatest challenges humanity has ever faced, and it is a challenge that will not be solved overnight.  In the meantime, we can stop our neighbors from suffering in the shadows. We are using data not just to observe a changing world, but to actively shield the people living in it.
