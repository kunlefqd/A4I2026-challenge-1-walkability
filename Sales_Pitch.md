Elevator Pitch

Heat waves don't hit cities evenly. One block can bake under a stationary heat dome while the air a few streets over stays bearable. And the people on the hottest blocks are often the least able to escape it: an elderly resident alone in a top-floor apartment with no air conditioning, a family that can't afford to run the AC all night, an outdoor worker with no shade, someone with no home at all.

City-wide heat alerts are too slow and too broad for that. Opening a cooling center doesn't help if the people who need it most are miles away, or if it's full or closed when they arrive.

SHADE, the Smart Heat Alert & Dispatch Engine, fixes that. It combines live weather data, heat index, overnight temperatures, urban heat-island readings, building age, power-outage reports, and socio-economic and community health data into one map. That map shows not just where it's hottest, but where heat is most likely to hurt someone.

When a heat spike starts to form, SHADE flags the highest-risk blocks [24 to 48 hours] before the first 911 call. Emergency teams can send hydration and medical units, route wellness-check volunteers to specific buildings, and redirect transit to shuttle residents to cooling hubs with room to take them. Limited resources go to the most exposed people first.

We can't stop the climate from changing overnight. But with SHADE, no neighbor has to face the heat alone.

Full Pitch

Summers have grown hotter and more erratic for years, but it was the sudden, localized heat spikes that demanded our attention. This was no longer a slow, general warming. Neighborhoods were baking under invisible, stationary heat domes while nearby streets stayed bearable. Nights stopped cooling down, so bodies had no chance to recover before the next day's heat. City-wide heat policies were too slow and too blunt to save lives in real time. We started this work because the margin between discomfort and tragedy had vanished, and the danger had become devastatingly precise.

When the mercury spikes and the grid strains under air-conditioning demand, the cost is paid by the most vulnerable among us. For an elderly resident living alone in a top-floor apartment without air conditioning, a three-day heat wave turns home into a trap. For low-income families, a sudden surge in energy bills forces an agonizing choice between food and air conditioning. For people with chronic illnesses, or on medications that weaken the body's ability to cool itself, a few degrees can mean the difference between a hard day and a hospital visit. For outdoor workers and people waiting at unshaded bus stops, there is no escape from the sun. And for the unhoused, extreme heat is an immediate, life-threatening crisis. In the micro-climates of asphalt, concrete, and systemic neglect, extreme heat isn't an inconvenience. It's a predator.

That is why we built SHADE, the Smart Heat Alert & Dispatch Engine. We knew that opening cooling centers wasn't enough if the people who needed them were miles away. SHADE combines real-time weather data, heat index and overnight lows, urban heat-island metrics, tree canopy and surface data, building age and insulation, live power-outage reports, and socio-economic and aggregated community health data, all handled with privacy safeguards. The result is a single map of a heat event as it unfolds. It doesn't just show where temperatures are breaking records; it shows where heat is threatening lives.

This lets us shift emergency response from reactive to proactive. When a heat spike begins to form, SHADE highlights the city blocks facing the highest combined risk, [24 to 48 hours] before the first emergency call. We can dispatch mobile hydration and medical units, route wellness-check volunteers to high-risk buildings and to residents who have opted into check-ins, and redirect transit to shuttle people to cooling hubs. Live capacity tracking and extended hours mean no one arrives to a full or locked door. When a block loses power, SHADE flags it immediately, because that is when the risk jumps. Our finite resources go directly to the hardest-hit, most exposed people first.

The same data guides the longer work too: where to plant trees, which roofs to cool, and where the next cooling center should open. The stakes are not hypothetical. In July 2026, Cook County reported 6 heat-related deaths, making it the county's deadliest July for heat in more than a decade.

Stabilizing the climate is one of the greatest challenges humanity has ever faced, and it won't be solved overnight. In the meantime, no neighbor should have to face the heat alone. We are using data not just to observe a changing world, but to protect the people living in it.
